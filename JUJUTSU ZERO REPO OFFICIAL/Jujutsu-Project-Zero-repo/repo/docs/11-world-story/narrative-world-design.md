# Narrative and World Design Framework

Status: DRAFT — ORIGINAL STORY DIRECTION
Purpose: Establish a dark, layered sorcery setting with Soulslike environmental storytelling and a JJK-inspired supernatural logic.
Last reviewed: 2026-11-23
Sources: Owner vision; GAME-ORIGINAL proposals; JJK canon is inspiration/reference only and must be attributed in the encyclopedia.
Related: [[../08-progression/soulslike-progression-design]], [[../02-jjk-encyclopedia/README]], [[../../MASTER_GAME_DESIGN]], [[../../PROJECT_RULES]]

## 1. Originality boundary

The project is JJK-inspired, but its playable story, characters, dialogue, factions, locations, quests, and history should be original. Canon characters and events may be discussed in the lore encyclopedia as reference material; do not silently insert them into the game's original timeline or present invented events as canon.

Use source labels:
- CANON-MANGA / CANON-ANIME for facts about the source material.
- INTERPRETATION for analysis.
- GAME-ORIGINAL for our setting and story.
Every story beat in this document is GAME-ORIGINAL unless explicitly stated otherwise.

## 2. Narrative promise

The player enters a society that has learned to manage supernatural catastrophe but has never understood its cause. Every institution claims to protect people. Each institution hides a different cost. The player gradually discovers that the current crisis is not a simple outbreak of monsters; it is the consequence of old bargains, suppressed history, and the way people turn fear into power.

Tone: eerie, intimate, tragic, occasionally brutal, and human. Use quiet scenes and ordinary details between spectacular fights. The world should feel inhabited before the player arrives.

## 3. Proposed setting seed (not yet owner-ratified)

A coastal city and its surrounding districts have been divided into containment zones after a series of "Quiet Nights" — nights when ordinary people disappear from memory, buildings change internally, and cursed phenomena manifest without a visible source.

The public explanation is infrastructure failure and mass hysteria. Sorcerer institutions call it a containment crisis. Independent practitioners believe the city is being used as a ritual instrument. Cursed spirits are not all mindless predators; some preserve fragments of the people and places that created them.

The player is not immediately the chosen one. They are a new operative, survivor, vessel, or anomaly whose first task is small: investigate one district and bring back proof that the official report is false.

This premise is a starting option. Claude must not treat it as locked canon until the owner approves it.

## 4. Story structure

### Act I — The surface story
- Establish the player's origin and immediate need.
- Introduce one mentor, one rival, one institutional authority, and one unreliable witness.
- Teach combat through a contained incident.
- End with a boss whose behavior contradicts the official explanation.

### Act II — Contradictions
- Open two or more districts with conflicting accounts.
- Let faction quests reveal different incentives.
- Introduce a cursed object / vessel mystery.
- Make a previously helpful NPC's motive understandable but questionable.
- Reveal that the player has agency in how to handle evidence.

### Act III — The buried mechanism
- Connect early environmental clues to a historical bargain or failed containment system.
- Recontextualize at least one boss and one faction.
- Let the player choose what to preserve, expose, or destroy.
- Avoid a single exposition dump explaining everything.

### Endgame — Consequence, not just bigger numbers
- Resolve major character arcs.
- Show consequences in locations and NPC behavior.
- Offer optional truths and challenge encounters.
- Endings should differ in values and consequences, not merely a good/evil meter.

## 5. Environmental storytelling

Every region should contain:
- one dominant visual motif,
- one safe or deceptively safe landmark,
- one environmental contradiction,
- one optional lore discovery,
- one NPC story told across multiple visits,
- one shortcut or altered route,
- one enemy whose design reflects the area's history,
- one boss whose arena completes the region's theme.

Use objects, sound, architecture, enemy placement, missing furniture, repeated symbols, and changed NPC dialogue. Do not rely only on collectible text logs.

## 6. Character writing

A memorable NPC needs:
- a want,
- a fear,
- a contradiction,
- a relationship,
- a secret,
- a voice pattern,
- a change after the player intervenes.

Do not write NPCs as quest vending machines. A character who asks the player to kill something should have a reason, and that reason may be incomplete or self-serving.

The rival should be competent and understandable, not evil solely to motivate the player. The mentor should teach useful skills but have blind spots. The authority figure should make a defensible argument for a harmful choice.

## 7. Boss narrative integration

A boss is a character, system, or historical consequence expressed through combat. Design the narrative and moveset together:
- what does it protect?
- what behavior does it punish?
- what is its tell language?
- what changes at each phase?
- what does the arena reveal?
- what does defeating it change in the world?
- what reward preserves its theme?

A boss should not be a random health sponge inserted to gate a level.

## 8. Lore delivery

Use three layers:
1. Immediate: environmental cues and NPC dialogue the player can understand now.
2. Investigative: optional documents, memories, item descriptions, and revisited spaces.
3. Recontextualizing: later discoveries that change the meaning of earlier events.

Every important fact should have a source-of-truth lore entry and a consistency note. Keep spoilers behind explicit spoiler headings. Record unresolved contradictions instead of silently choosing one.

## 9. Factions and origins

Factions should differ in worldview, not only uniforms and stat bonuses. Each needs:
- public doctrine,
- private incentive,
- internal disagreement,
- historical responsibility,
- recruitment test,
- signature combat philosophy,
- reason a player might leave or oppose it.

Origins must have different narrative and mechanical paths but comparable long-term viability. A rare origin can feel unusual without becoming the best competitive choice.

## 10. Story-to-gameplay integration

Story choices should affect something concrete:
- a route opens or closes,
- an NPC changes location or dialogue,
- a boss gains or loses an optional phase,
- a mentor teaches a different technique variant,
- a faction's services change,
- a world event changes its enemy roster.

Do not promise branching choices that require huge content production unless scope is approved. Prefer a few meaningful, trackable flags over dozens of shallow dialogue branches.

## 11. Writing constraints

- No exposition monologues longer than the player can reasonably absorb in a single interaction.
- No generic "ancient evil awakens" without a specific cause and human consequence.
- Avoid lore that exists only to justify a new boss.
- Do not copy recognizable Soulsborne characters, item descriptions, faction names, or quest structures.
- Keep canon JJK references clearly separated from GAME-ORIGINAL story facts.

## Implementation notes
Start with one district, four named NPCs, three enemy archetypes, one elite, one boss, one hub, and one mystery with a reveal. Expand only after that slice works.

## Testing criteria
- [ ] A player can explain the immediate stakes without reading a lore wiki.
- [ ] Optional discoveries add meaning, not required stats.
- [ ] At least one early clue gains new meaning later.
- [ ] Boss identity is visible in both story and moveset.
- [ ] Factions have understandable competing motives.
- [ ] All canon claims are labelled and sourced.
